Unifying the CJKV Matrix

The Overdue Hán-Việt Patch — Cross-lingual inheritance strategies for completing the Sino-Vietnamese phonetic layer.

“One glyph, four voices. No common Han character left behind.”

Abstract

The digital infrastructure of East Asian languages contains a massive, historically overlooked blind spot. While Chinese, Japanese, and Korean character readings have been rigorously standardized for modern computation, the Sino-Vietnamese (Hán-Việt) layer remains frozen, incomplete, and structurally flawed within the Unicode Standard (Unihan). Because historical contributions conflated colloquial native words (Nôm) with scholarly pronunciations (Hán-Việt), modern cross-lingual tools are left with a broken dataset. This paper outlines a methodology to finally deploy the “overdue patch”—utilizing variant inheritance, Cantonese phonetic bridging, and traditional mapping to build a complete, 1:1 CJKV conversion matrix.

中日韓越の漢字体系の統合：長らく見過ごされてきた「Hán-Việt音」データの完全補完

東アジア言語のデジタル基盤には、歴史的に大きく見落とされてきた深刻な死角が存在します。中国語、日本語、韓国語における漢字の読みが、現代のコンピューター処理に向けて厳格に標準化されてきた一方で、Unicode標準（Unihanデータベース）におけるベトナム語の漢字音（漢越音：Hán-Việt）のデータは更新が止まり、不完全かつ構造的な欠陥を抱えたままになっています。

この問題の根本は、過去のデータ登録において、ベトナムの日常的な固有語（チュノム：Nôm）と、学術的な漢字音（漢越音：Hán-Việt）が混同されたことにあります。その結果、現代の言語間ツールは破綻したデータセットに依存せざるを得ない状況です。本稿では、異体字の継承関係、広東語との音声的な架け橋、そして繁体字とのマッピングを活用することで、完全な「1対1」の中日韓越（CJKV）漢字変換体系を構築する道筋を示します。

Thống Nhất Ma Trận CJKV: Bản vá Hán-Việt muộn màng nhưng thiết yếu

Cơ sở hạ tầng kỹ thuật số của các ngôn ngữ Đông Á hiện đang còn tồn tại một điểm mù khổng lồ bị lịch sử bỏ quên. Trong khi âm đọc chữ Hán của tiếng Trung, tiếng Nhật và tiếng Hàn đã được tiêu chuẩn hóa một cách nghiêm ngặt để phục vụ cho điện toán hiện đại, thì phân lớp ngữ âm Hán-Việt lại bị đóng băng, thiếu sót và mang khiếm khuyết về mặt cấu trúc ngay bên trong Tiêu chuẩn Unicode (cơ sở dữ liệu Unihan).

Do các đóng góp dữ liệu trong quá khứ đã đánh đồng từ vựng tiếng mẹ đẻ thông dụng (chữ Nôm) với các âm đọc học thuật (Hán-Việt), các công cụ chuyển đổi đa ngôn ngữ hiện nay đang phải gánh chịu một bộ dữ liệu chắp vá và sai lệch. Bài nghiên cứu này phác thảo một phương pháp nhằm chính thức triển khai “bản vá” dữ liệu bị bỏ ngỏ từ lâu này, tận dụng quy tắc kế thừa dị thể (異體繼承規則), cầu nối ngữ âm tiếng Quảng Đông và cơ chế đối chiếu phồn thể để xây dựng một ma trận chuyển đổi Trung-Nhật-Hàn-Việt (CJKV) 1:1 hoàn chỉnh.

§1

The Problem

SINO-CONV uses Unicode’s Unihan database as its source for character-level pronunciation readings. The kVietnamese field provides Sino-Vietnamese readings — but its coverage is far behind the other four languages. The Unihan database editors (Jenkins, Cook, Lunde) explicitly note in UAX #38 that fields like kVietnamese are “provisional” and built from disparate historical dictionary submissions, unlike kMandarin which is heavily standardized.

§2

The Nôm vs Hán-Việt Discovery

After forking KanjiDictVN (10,383 entries from hvdic.thivien.net) and cross-referencing against Unihan, a critical finding emerged:

Unihan’s kVietnamese field stores Nôm / colloquial Vietnamese readings, not Sino-Vietnamese (Hán-Việt) readings. KanjiDictVN stores proper Hán-Việt. These are fundamentally different reading layers of the same characters.

For SINO-CONV’s purpose — building a 1:1 comparative matrix across CJKV — Hán-Việt is the only functionally correct layer. It is the direct parallel to on’yomi (Japanese), hangul readings (Korean), and pīnyīn (Mandarin). All four are phonological descendants of Middle Chinese. The Nôm readings are valuable for Chữ Nôm decipherment, but that is a different project entirely.

Nôm Reading = Kun’yomi: When Unihan applies the colloquial native Vietnamese word vú to 乳, it is doing the exact same thing Japanese does when reading 乳 as the native chichi (kun’yomi) — mapping a native spoken word onto a borrowed foreign symbol based purely on meaning.

Hán-Việt = On’yomi: When KanjiDictVN logs the scholarly reading nhũ for 乳, it is what Japanese does when reading it as nyû (on’yomi). Both are direct phonological descendants of the original Middle Chinese pronunciation.

Sources: Lunde, CJKV Information Processing (O’Reilly, 2008); Nguyễn, in Daniels & Bright eds., The World’s Writing Systems (OUP, 1996); DeFrancis, The Chinese Language: Fact and Fantasy (Univ. Hawaii Press, 1984).

§3

The Dị Thể / Biến Thể Gap

Some missing entries follow a pattern: Unihan maps the traditional form but not its simplified or shinjitai variant. These are dị thể (異體 — variant forms) or biến thể (變體 — transformed forms) — characters where Japan or China reformed the glyph but the meaning and pronunciation are identical.

Under Unicode’s Han Unification and the Source Separation Rule (ISO/IEC 10646), if two countries used different glyphs for the same character (e.g., 學 vs 学), Unicode assigns them separate code points to prevent data loss. But linguistically, they are the exact same word. SINO-CONV safely copies the traditional reading over to the simplified glyph.

TraditionalkVietnameseVariantkVietnameseRelationship
變 U+8B8A biến ✓ 変 U+5909 MISSING ✗ JP shinjitai
變 U+8B8A biến ✓ 变 U+53D8 MISSING ✗ CN simplified
學 U+5B78 học ✓ 学 U+5B66 ? JP shinjitai / CN simplified

Strategy: Cross-reference variant chains. If a simplified or shinjitai character has no kVietnamese entry, but its traditional source form does — we can confidently inherit the reading. OpenCC already maps these relationships.

Accuracy estimate pending — need to measure how many gaps this fills and what percentage are clean 1:1 mappings vs. split readings.

Caveat: Many-to-One mergers. Mainland Chinese simplification collapsed multiple traditional characters into single simplified forms. Example: the simplified 面 (miàn) maps to both 面 (face, Hán-Việt: diện) and 麵 (noodles, Hán-Việt: miến). If the script grabs the first mapping in OpenCC, it may assign an incomplete reading. Characters with multiple traditional sources must be flagged for manual review.

Expanding the Fix to Japanese and Korean

This variant gap is not exclusive to Vietnamese. Because South Korea historically uses Traditional Hanja and Japan uses Shinjitai, Unihan frequently lacks kHangul and kJapaneseOn readings for Mainland Simplified characters or cross-border variants.

§4

The Cantonese–Vietnamese Correlation

Both Cantonese (Quảng Đông) and Sino-Vietnamese (Hán-Việt) heavily preserved the phonetic structure of Late Middle Chinese (approx. 7th–10th century). While Mandarin eventually merged or dropped its final consonants (-p, -t, -k, -m), Cantonese and Vietnamese kept them. This makes Cantonese a highly accurate phonetic predictor for missing Vietnamese readings.

Why are Cantonese and Vietnamese so similar? Both Cantonese (Yue) and Hán-Việt heavily preserved the phonetic structure of Late Middle Chinese. While Mandarin eventually merged or dropped its final consonants (-p, -t, -k, -m), Cantonese and Vietnamese kept them. Cantonese words ending in -k almost always end in -c or -ch in Vietnamese. This makes Cantonese a highly accurate phonetic predictor for missing Vietnamese readings.

Sources: Pulleyblank, Lexicon of Reconstructed Pronunciation in Early Middle Chinese, Late Middle Chinese, and Early Mandarin (UBC Press, 1991); Alves, Exploring Statistical Regularities in Sino-Vietnamese Loanmorph Phonology (2022); Wang Li (Vương Lực), Hán-Việt ngữ âm nghiên cứu / 漢越語研究 (1948).

Strategy: Cantonese as a bridge. Unihan’s kCantonese field has far better coverage than kVietnamese. For common glyphs that have a Cantonese reading but no Vietnamese one, the Cantonese pronunciation can serve as a phonetic guide to predict or verify the missing Hán-Việt reading.

This is a secondary fallback — thivien.net data is preferred when available. Cantonese bridging helps for cross-verification and confidence scoring.

Plan

Phase 1 — Fork & Merge

  • Fork KanjiDictVN data (~10,350 chars) as baseline expansion
  • Query thivien.net API for CJK Unified Ideographs range (U+4E00–U+9FFF, ~21K chars)
  • Merge with existing Unihan kVietnamese data, preferring Hán-Việt where both exist
  • Store merged data locally in research/data/

Phase 2 — Error Scan

  • Cross-verify thivien.net readings against Unihan where overlapping
  • Flag discrepancies for manual review
  • Focus on common characters (HSK lists, JLPT kanji, frequent-use tables)
  • Side-by-side comparison tables published here

Phase 3 — Variant Auto-fill

  • Map simplified → traditional → kVietnamese via OpenCC conversion tables
  • Map shinjitai → kyūjitai → kVietnamese
  • Apply this identical inheritance logic to patch missing kHangul and kJapaneseOn readings for variant glyphs
  • Calculate accuracy rate: what % of variant→traditional mappings are clean 1:1 reading inheritance
  • Auto-fill only where confidence is high; flag ambiguous cases (Many-to-One mergers)

Phase 4 — Cantonese Bridge

  • For remaining gaps: use Unihan kCantonese as phonetic predictor
  • Build Cantonese→Hán-Việt correspondence table from known pairs
  • Predict missing readings with confidence scores
  • Manual verification for predicted entries

Scope

Goal: Full Hán-Việt coverage for all common modern Simplified and Traditional Chinese characters, plus common Japanese kanji. Additionally, SINO-CONV will patch missing Japanese (on’yomi) and Korean (hangul) readings for Simplified and Shinjitai variants, creating a fully bridged CJKV matrix. We are not aiming to cover all obsolete glyphs or the full Chữ Nôm repertoire — that is beyond SINO-CONV’s current scope.

If we cover the 13,175 entries in kJapaneseOn, we are covering far more than modern use. For context: Jōyō Kanji (常用漢字) is only 2,136 characters; Jinmeiyō Kanji (人名用漢字) adds 863; Kanji Kentei Level 1 tests ~6,000. Korean’s 8,411 entries map to the KS X 1001/1002 national standards plus the Supreme Court name Hanja list — legally complete. Neither Korea nor Vietnam imported new Chinese glyphs for modern concepts; instead they adopted Japan’s wasei-kango (和製漢語) compound words and read them in their local pronunciations (e.g., 電話 → điện thoại / 전화 / diànhuà).
